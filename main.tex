\title{Co-Dimensional coupling with FESTIM}

\author[mit]{James Dark}
\author[umn]{Ian Wojtowicz}
\author[mit]{Remi Delaporte-Mathurin}

\address[mit]{Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Cambridge, MA, USA}
\address[umn]{Department of Mechanical Engineering, University of Minnesota, 111 Church Street SE, Minneapolis, MN 55455, USA}

\begin{abstract}
Thin features such as material interfaces, grain boundaries and coolant channels often control hydrogen transport in fusion components.
However, they are costly to resolve in a mesh.
This work presents a co-dimensional description of such features in the open-source hydrogen transport code \FESTIM{}.
The feature is a subdomain of one dimension less than the mesh, called a manifold.
It carries its own transport equation and exchanges a flux with the bulk.
The manifold and the bulk are solved together in a single nonlinear system.
The implementation converges at second order against a manufactured solution, in 2D and on a tilted manifold in 3D.
Three applications are presented.
A permeator tube with the flowing liquid, carried on a manifold, reproduces the published extraction efficiencies of \num{0.690} and \num{0.415}.
An interface carrying its own inventory reproduces molecular dynamics results at Be/BeO and a multi-site rate-equation model at W/Cu.
For a W/Cu bilayer, the interfacial inventory leaves the steady flux unchanged.
However, it delays breakthrough by more than four orders of magnitude in thin layers at low temperature.
Finally, the grain-boundary network of a Voronoi polycrystal is described as a single manifold, with no condition required at triple junctions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ===============================================================

Hydrogen isotope transport in materials governs fuel retention, permeation losses and tritium accountancy in fusion systems~\cite{meschini_impact_2025, meschini_modeling_2023}.
Predicting these quantities at the component scale relies on macroscopic transport codes such as \FESTIM{}~\cite{dark_festim_2026}, \TMAP{}8~\cite{simon_moose-based_2025}, \textsc{tessim-x}~\cite{schmid_implications_2024} and \MHIMS{}~\cite{hodille_macroscopic_2015},
which solve diffusion and trapping equations on a meshed domain.

Fusion components contain features that are far thinner than the components themselves.
Examples include the interfaces between materials, grain boundaries, cracks and coolant channels.
These features often control the transport of hydrogen.
For example, an interface can trap hydrogen and delay permeation~\cite{hodille_molecular_2022, silva-solis_hydrogen_2026}, a grain boundary can act as a fast diffusion path \TODO{citation needed},  and a flowing liquid carries away what permeates into it.
However, resolving such a feature in the mesh of a larger component is costly.
It drives both the number of cells and their aspect ratio.

The existing treatments avoid this cost by removing the feature from the model, and each discards something in doing so.
A homogenised property loses the state of the feature.
An algebraic interface condition loses the inventory of the interface and its kinetics.
Coupling to an external system code loses the monolithic solution of the fluid and the solid.
These limitations are detailed in Sec.~\ref{sec:motivation}.

To address them, this work presents a co-dimensional description of thin features in \FESTIM{}.
The feature is represented as a subdomain of one dimension less than the mesh, called a manifold.
It carries its own transport equation and exchanges a flux with the bulk.
The formulation, its implementation and its verification are described in Sec.~\ref{sec:method}.
It is then applied to three cases: a permeator tube with the flowing liquid carried on a manifold (Sec.~\ref{sec:app:pfr}), an interface carrying its own inventory, at Be/BeO and at W/Cu (Sec.~\ref{sec:app:interface}), and a grain-boundary network in a polycrystal (Sec.~\ref{sec:app:gb}).
The second case extends a companion paper, in which interfaces are treated as reactive surfaces with no stored inventory. \TODO{cite the companion once it has a number}

The implementation is available in \FESTIM{} 2.2.
All the results presented in this paper can be reproduced with the scripts archived on Zenodo~\cite{james_dark_2026_24349184} and described in Sec.~\ref{sec:data}.

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}
% ===============================================================

The features considered here are separated in scale from the components that contain them.
An interfacial plane or a grain boundary is of atomic width, inside a component of millimetre scale. \TODO{representative widths, with citations}
A mesh that resolves such a feature pays for it along its whole extent.
Three treatments are commonly used to avoid this.

The first is homogenisation, in which the feature is folded into an effective diffusivity of the surrounding material \cite{hart_diffusion_1957, harrison_diffusion_1961, oriani_diffusion_1970}.
The concentration in the feature is then no longer a variable of the model.
Neither the inventory held in the feature nor the flux carried along it can be reported.

The second is an algebraic interface condition, in which continuity of chemical potential is imposed on the two bulk concentrations. \TODO{Citation}
The interface then holds no inventory and has no time scale of its own.
However, molecular dynamics simulations of the Be/BeO interface show trapping sites localised at the interface, which fill over a finite time~\cite{hodille_molecular_2022}.
Rate-equation modelling of the W/Cu interface reaches the same conclusion~\cite{silva-solis_hydrogen_2026}.

The third is external coupling, in which a flowing fluid is handed to a separate system code that exchanges boundary data with the component model~\cite{meschini_modeling_2023}.
The coupling is then explicit, and the two models do not share a time step.

A manifold keeps what these treatments discard.
It has its own field, its own transport terms, boundary conditions and exports, and it is solved together with the bulk.
Mixed-dimensional coupling of this kind is established in other fields, for instance for flow in fractured porous media. \TODO{cite: D'Angelo and Quarteroni; Boon, Nordbotten and Yotov; Kuchta et al.}
To our knowledge, it has not been available in a hydrogen transport code alongside trapping and multi-species reactions. \VERIFY{}

\section{Co-dimensional modelling in \FESTIM{}}
\label{sec:method}
% ===============================================================

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Governing equations and coupling to the bulk}
\label{sec:method:governing}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

Consider a domain of dimension $d$ divided into bulk subdomains $\Omega_i$, and a manifold $\Gamma$ of dimension $d-1$ lying on their facets.
The manifold may lie on the outer boundary, inside one subdomain, between two subdomains or between many.
It may also end inside the domain (see Fig.~\ref{fig:illustration}).
In each bulk subdomain, the transport of mobile hydrogen is unchanged from \FESTIM{}~\cite{dark_festim_2026}:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial c_i}{\partial t} = \nabla \cdot \left( D_i \nabla c_i \right) + S_i \quad \text{in } \Omega_i,
    \label{eq:bulk}
\end{equation}
where $c_i$ is the mobile concentration, $D_i$ is the diffusivity and $S_i$ is a source term on the $i$th subdomain $\Omega_i$.
Trapping and reactions are available as usual and are omitted here for brevity.

\begin{figure*}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{illustration}
    \caption{Illustration of a manifold $\Gamma$ with a diffusivity 200 times that of the bulk, embedded in a 2D domain loaded on its left edge: concentration fields (a) and profiles along $y = 0.5$ (b). The quantities are dimensionless.}
    \label{fig:illustration}
\end{figure*}

The manifold carries the same balance, written with the tangential operator:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial c_\Gamma}{\partial t} + \gradG \cdot \left( \mathbf{u}\, c_\Gamma \right) = \gradG \cdot \left( D_\Gamma \gradG c_\Gamma \right) + S_\Gamma + \alpha \sum_i J_i \quad \text{on } \Gamma
    \label{eq:manifold}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \gradG = \left( \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{n} \otimes \mathbf{n} \right) \nabla
    \label{eq:gradG}
\end{equation}
where $c_\Gamma$ is the concentration on the manifold, $D_\Gamma$ is its diffusivity, $\mathbf{u}$ is a drift velocity and $S_\Gamma$ is a source term.
$\mathbf{n}$ is the unit normal to $\Gamma$, $\textbf{I}$ is a $d$-dimensional identity matrix, and $J_i$ is the flux received from $\Omega_i$.
The sum runs over the bulk subdomains adjacent to $\Gamma$.
Equation~\ref{eq:gradG} removes the normal component of the gradient, so only the tangential part of $\mathbf{u}$ acts on the manifold.

The flux that enters the manifold leaves the bulk:
\begin{equation}
    -D_i \nabla c_i \cdot \mathbf{n}_i = J_i \left( c_i, c_\Gamma \right) \quad \text{on } \Gamma
    \label{eq:coupling}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{n}_i$ is the normal pointing out of $\Omega_i$.
No condition links the bulk concentrations on the two sides of $\Gamma$ directly.
They are independent and communicate only through $c_\Gamma$.
The exchange law $J_i$ is supplied by the user, one per adjacent subdomain.
The simplest is linear, $J_i = k_i \left( c_i - c_\Gamma \right)$, and the applications below use mass-transfer, trapping and detrapping laws.

The exchange flux $J_i$ is expressed per unit area of $\Gamma$.
The factor $\alpha$ in Eq.~\ref{eq:manifold} converts it to the units of $c_\Gamma$, and its value is a modelling choice.
For an areal density, such as the population of an interfacial plane, $\alpha = 1$.
For a volumetric concentration in a layer of thickness $\lambda$, $\alpha = 1/\lambda$.
For a channel of wetted perimeter $P$ and flow area $A$, $\alpha = P/A$.
\FESTIM{} does not impose this choice, so the value used is stated with each application.

With Eqs.~\ref{eq:manifold} and~\ref{eq:coupling}, the total inventory $\sum_i \int_{\Omega_i} c_i + \alpha^{-1} \int_\Gamma c_\Gamma$ changes only through sources and outer boundaries.
When the exchange is fast and the capacity of $\Gamma$ is negligible, $c_\Gamma$ follows the bulk concentrations and an algebraic interface condition is recovered.
This limit is derived for an interface in Sec.~\ref{sec:app:interface:setup}.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Weak formulation and mixed-dimensional assembly}
\label{sec:method:weak}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

Each subdomain has its own function space, defined on its own submesh: the cells of $\Omega_i$ and the facets of $\Gamma$.
First-order Lagrange elements are used throughout.
With test functions $v_i$ and $w$, and omitting outer boundary terms, the weak forms of Eqs.~\ref{eq:bulk} and ~\ref{eq:manifold} read:
\begin{equation}
    \int_{\Omega_i} \left( \frac{\partial c_i}{\partial t} v_i + D_i \nabla c_i \cdot \nabla v_i - S_i v_i \right) \mathrm{d}x + \int_\Gamma J_i\, v_i \,\mathrm{d}s = 0
    \label{eq:weak:bulk}
\end{equation}
\begin{multline}
    \int_\Gamma \left( \frac{\partial c_\Gamma}{\partial t} w + \left( D_\Gamma \gradG c_\Gamma - \mathbf{u}\, c_\Gamma \right) \cdot \gradG w - S_\Gamma w \right) \mathrm{d}s \\
    - \alpha \sum_i \int_\Gamma J_i\, w \,\mathrm{d}s = 0
    \label{eq:weak:manifold}
\end{multline}
The drift term of Eq.~\ref{eq:weak:manifold} is integrated by parts.
An end of a manifold with no boundary condition applied therefore carries zero total flux, and an outlet requires an explicit outflow condition.
Time derivatives are discretised with the backward Euler scheme, as in the rest of \FESTIM{}.

The two integrals of Eq.~\ref{eq:weak:manifold} require different integration domains.
The tangential terms are integrated on the submesh of $\Gamma$, where $\gradG$ is defined.
The exchange terms are integrated on the facets of the parent mesh, where the bulk fields can be evaluated.
\textsc{dolfinx}~\cite{baratta_dolfinx_2023} compiles one integration domain per form.
The residual is therefore split into groups, which are accumulated into a single vector and a single matrix \cite{dokken_scifem_2025}. \TODO{acknowledge the assembly callbacks adapted from J. S. Dokken}
One Newton solve is performed per time step, with no sub-iteration between the manifold and the bulk.

Inside the mesh, $\Gamma$ is integrated over interior facets, and each exchange term is restricted to one side.
One exchange is therefore declared per adjacent subdomain.
Beyond two adjacent subdomains, there is no consistent pair of sides, since the manifold separates a different pair past a triple junction.
In this case, each adjacent subdomain is given its own integral, on which it is the restricted side.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{\FESTIM{} user API}
\label{sec:method:api}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

A manifold is declared as a \texttt{VolumeSubdomain} whose dimension is one less than that of the mesh.
The exchange of Eq.~\ref{eq:coupling} is written twice, as a flux on the bulk species and as a source on the manifold species:
\begin{python}
import festim as F

gamma = F.VolumeSubdomain(
    id=2, dim=1, material=mat_gamma,
    locator=lambda x: np.isclose(x[0], 0))
c = F.Species("c", subdomains=[bulk])
c_g = F.Species("c_g", subdomains=[gamma])
both = {"c": c, "c_g": c_g}
J = lambda c, c_g: k * (c - c_g)

flux = F.ParticleFluxBC(
    subdomain=gamma, species=c,
    value=lambda c, c_g: -J(c, c_g),
    species_dependent_value=both)
source = F.ParticleSource(
    volume=gamma, species=c_g,
    value=lambda c, c_g: alpha * J(c, c_g),
    species_dependent_value=both)
\end{python}
Here a line in a 2D mesh exchanges a linear flux with the bulk subdomain \texttt{bulk}.
Both objects read the two concentrations, which live on different submeshes.

A manifold supports several species, reactions between them, trapping and drift terms.
Fixed concentration and outflow conditions can be applied on its ends.
The exports include totals and averages over the manifold, the exchange flux on its facets and the flux through its ends.
Cartesian, cylindrical and spherical coordinates are supported, and in a 1D mesh the manifold is a point.
A pair of subdomains is separated either by an algebraic interface condition or by a manifold, never both.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Verification}
\label{sec:method:verification}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The implementation is verified with the method of manufactured solutions on a unit square and unit cube, with $\Gamma$ being one edge and one face of the domain, respectively..
With a linear exchange $J = k \left( c - c_\Gamma \right)$, the exact solution is chosen as:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        c &= 1 + \xi_0^2 + \left( 1 + \xi_0 \right) \cos \left( \pi \xi_1 \right) \\
        c_\Gamma &= 1 + \left( 1 - \frac{D}{k} \right) \cos \left( \pi \xi_1 \right)
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:mms}
\end{equation}
where $\left( \xi_0, \xi_1 \right)$ are coordinates ofo the domain, with $\Gamma$ at $\xi_0 = 0$.
The sources follow from Eqs.~\ref{eq:bulk} and~\ref{eq:manifold}.
The exchange flux, $J = D \cos \left( \pi \xi_1 \right)$, varies along $\Gamma$.
In 3D, the cube is rotated so that $\Gamma$ is aligned with no coordinate axis, which exercises Eq.~\ref{eq:gradG}.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{convergence}
    \caption{Relative $L^2$ error of the bulk and manifold concentrations against the manufactured solution of Eq.~\ref{eq:mms}, in 2D and on a tilted manifold in 3D.}
    \label{fig:convergence}
\end{figure}

The relative $L^2$ error of both fields decreases at second order, as expected for first-order finite elements (see Fig.~\ref{fig:convergence}).
The fitted orders are \num{2.00} in 2D and \num{1.97} in 3D.
The \FESTIM{} test suite additionally covers conservation across an interior manifold, independence from the order of declaration, triple junctions and radial coordinates.

\section{Application 1: coupling a 1D flow model to a 2D component}
\label{sec:app:pfr}
% ===============================================================

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Model setup}
\label{sec:app:pfr:setup}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The first application is a permeator tube, in which tritium dissolved in a flowing liquid permeates through the tube wall into vacuum.
The wall is an axisymmetric bulk subdomain.
The liquid is not meshed.
It is a manifold on the inner surface of the wall, at radius $r_\mathrm{i}$, carrying the cross-section averaged concentration $c_\mathrm{f}$.
This concentration obeys a plug-flow balance, which is Eq.~\ref{eq:manifold} with $\alpha = 2/r_\mathrm{i}$ and a flux leaving the liquid:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial c_\mathrm{f}}{\partial t} + u \frac{\partial c_\mathrm{f}}{\partial z} = D_\mathrm{l} \frac{\partial^2 c_\mathrm{f}}{\partial z^2} - \frac{2}{r_\mathrm{i}} J
    \label{eq:pfr}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    J = k_\mathrm{T} \left( c_\mathrm{f} - c_\mathrm{w,l} \right)
    \label{eq:pfr:J}
\end{equation}
where $u$ is the mean velocity, $D_\mathrm{l}$ is the diffusivity in the liquid and $k_\mathrm{T}$ is the mass transfer coefficient.
$c_\mathrm{w,l}$ is the concentration in the liquid at the wall, which is assumed to be in equilibrium with the metal surface at concentration $c_\mathrm{w}$:
\begin{equation}
    c_\mathrm{w,l} =
    \begin{cases}
        \dfrac{K_\mathrm{l}}{K_\mathrm{s}}\, c_\mathrm{w} & \text{Sieverts liquid} \\[2ex]
        K_\mathrm{H} \left( \dfrac{c_\mathrm{w}}{K_\mathrm{s}} \right)^2 & \text{Henry liquid}
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:pfr:eq}
\end{equation}
where $K_\mathrm{s}$ and $K_\mathrm{l}$ are the Sieverts solubilities of the wall and of the liquid, and $K_\mathrm{H}$ is a Henry solubility.
The second law makes the exchange nonlinear in the wall concentration.

A fixed concentration $c_\mathrm{in}$ is applied at the inlet of the manifold and an outflow condition at its outlet.
The concentration is set to zero on the outer surface of the wall.
Two published cases are considered: Pb-17Li in a RAFM steel tube, and FLiBe in a nickel tube \cite{rader_extraction_2018}. 
Their parameters are given in Table~\ref{tab:permeator}.
For a Sieverts liquid, the steady extraction efficiency $\eta = 1 - c_\mathrm{f}(L)/c_\mathrm{in}$ of a tube of length $L$ and inner diameter $d$ has an analytical solution: \TODO{cite Humrickhouse and Merrill 2015}
\begin{equation}
    \begin{gathered}
        \eta = 1 - \exp \left( -\tau \frac{\zeta}{1 + \zeta} \right) \\
        \tau = \frac{4 k_\mathrm{T} L}{u\, d}, \qquad \zeta = \frac{2 D_\mathrm{s} K_\mathrm{s}}{k_\mathrm{T} K_\mathrm{l}\, d \ln \left( d_\mathrm{o}/d \right)}
    \end{gathered}
    \label{eq:pfr:eta}
\end{equation}
where $D_\mathrm{s}$ is the diffusivity in the wall and $d_\mathrm{o}$ is the outer diameter.
The Henry case also has an analytical solution, expressed with the Lambert $W$ function.

\section{Application 2: interfaces carrying their own inventory}
\label{sec:app:interface}
% ===============================================================

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Model setup}
\label{sec:app:interface:setup}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

In the second application, the plane between two materials is a manifold with $\alpha = 1$.
Its concentration $c_\Gamma$ is an areal density on $n_\Gamma$ sites per unit area, with coverage $\theta = c_\Gamma / n_\Gamma$.
The exchange with material $i$ consists of a trapping jump from the bulk and a detrapping jump back:
\begin{equation}
    J_i = \nu_{i \rightarrow \Gamma}\, \lambda_i\, c_i \left( 1 - \theta \right) - \nu_{\Gamma \rightarrow i}\, c_\Gamma
    \label{eq:interface:J}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial c_\Gamma}{\partial t} = J_1 + J_2
    \label{eq:interface:balance}
\end{equation}
where $\nu$ are jump frequencies following an Arrhenius law and $\lambda_i$ is the jump distance in material $i$.
The product $\lambda_i c_i$ is the areal density of bulk atoms within reach of the interface.
At steady state with no net flux and $\theta \ll 1$, Eqs.~\ref{eq:interface:J} and~\ref{eq:interface:balance} give \TODO{cite companion paper?}:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{c_2}{c_1} = \frac{\nu_{1 \rightarrow \Gamma}\, \lambda_1\, \nu_{\Gamma \rightarrow 2}}{\nu_{\Gamma \rightarrow 1}\, \nu_{2 \rightarrow \Gamma}\, \lambda_2}
    \label{eq:interface:lte}
\end{equation}
Equation~\ref{eq:interface:lte} is the algebraic equilibrium condition to which the manifold reduces.
It retains neither the inventory $c_\Gamma$ nor the time required to fill it.

Three cases are considered.
The first is the Be/BeO interface, for which Hodille et al. simulated deuterium trapping by molecular dynamics at \qtylist{750;1000;1250}{\kelvin}~\cite{hodille_molecular_2022}.
No exchange was observed on the BeO side, so $J_2 = 0$.
The jump frequencies, $n_\Gamma = \qty{e20}{\per\metre\squared}$ and a beryllium concentration of \qty{5e27}{\per\metre\cubed} are taken from that reference.
The interface is not empty at the start of the molecular dynamics record, so $c_\Gamma$ starts from the value of the first frame.

The second case is the multi-site model of the W/Cu interface of Silva-Sol\'is et al.~\cite{silva-solis_hydrogen_2026}. \VERIFY{cite the thesis for the tables used}
It contains fourteen sites across the interface, joined by three diffusion paths, with barriers obtained from density functional theory.
Here, the fourteen sites are fourteen species on one manifold, and each jump between two sites is a pair of opposite sources.
The concentration on the tungsten side is held at \qty{2.3e7}{\per\metre\cubed}, as in the reference.

The third case places the interface between a tungsten layer and a copper layer in 1D.
The multi-site path is reduced to Eq.~\ref{eq:interface:J}, and the result is compared with Eq.~\ref{eq:interface:lte} imposed as an interface condition. \VERIFY{the reduction is ours; refit its attempt frequency to the multi-site model}
The copper layer is one sixth of the tungsten thickness $L_\mathrm{W}$.
A concentration of \qty{e20}{\per\metre\cubed} is held on the tungsten surface and zero on the copper surface.
It should be noted that nothing in these cases is fitted, and that the third is not compared with a measurement.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Model setup}
\label{sec:app:gb:setup}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\TODO{Include a comparison to the analytical approximations derived by Fisher/Hart?}

Grain boundaries can act as fast diffusion paths for hydrogen \cite{fisher_grain_boundary_1951, diaz-rodriguez_direct_2022}. \TODO{cite grain-boundary diffusion data}
The third application considers a 2D Voronoi tessellation of 30 grains in a square of side $L$ \cite{virtanen_scipy_2020}.
It is meshed so that every boundary lies on facets.
Each grain is a bulk subdomain with its own concentration $c_g$.
The whole boundary network is one manifold, adjacent to every grain, with a single field.
The boundaries have no thickness: the network carries an areal density $\sigma$, with $\alpha = 1$:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \frac{\partial \sigma}{\partial t} &= \gradG \cdot \left( D_\mathrm{gb} \gradG \sigma \right) + \sum_g J_g \\
        J_g &= k_\mathrm{t}\, c_g - k_\mathrm{d}\, \sigma
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:gb}
\end{equation}
where $D_\mathrm{gb}$ is the diffusivity along the boundaries, $k_\mathrm{t}$ is a trapping velocity and $k_\mathrm{d}$ is a detrapping rate.
The ratio $k_\mathrm{t}/k_\mathrm{d}$ is a segregation length.

Since one field lives on a connected network, hydrogen crosses a triple junction with no junction condition to write.
Hydrogen passes from one grain to the next only through the boundary between them.
A unit concentration is applied on one face of the square, to the grains and to the ends of the boundaries in equilibrium with them.
The concentration is set to zero on the opposite face.
The case is dimensionless, with $D_\mathrm{gb}/D = 10^{3}$, $k_\mathrm{t} L / D = 10^{2}$ and $k_\mathrm{t}/(k_\mathrm{d} L) = 10^{-2}$.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ===============================================================

The three applications use Eqs.~\ref{eq:manifold} and~\ref{eq:coupling} unchanged.
Only the exchange law $J_i$ and the factor $\alpha$ differ between a flowing liquid, an interfacial plane and a grain-boundary network.
A manifold is appropriate when the feature is thin compared with the scale over which the bulk concentration varies.
The feature must also carry its own state or its own transport.
It is not appropriate when the profile across the feature matters.

The manifold contains the algebraic interface condition as the limit of fast exchange and negligible capacity (see Eq.~\ref{eq:interface:lte}).
The two descriptions give the same steady flux, and differ in the transient by the time needed to fill the interface.
Section~\ref{sec:app:interface:results} shows that this time is negligible for millimetre layers of W/Cu and dominant for micrometre layers at \qty{340}{\kelvin}.
Similarly, the manifold reproduces the plug-flow solution of a system code, and adds the transient and a resolved wall in the same solve.

It should be noted that the factor $\alpha$ is left to the user.
An inconsistent choice gives a model that runs and conserves the wrong quantity, so it is a likely source of error.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Limitations and future work}
\label{sec:discussion:limitations}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

Only codimension one is supported.
A line in a 3D mesh is excluded, since a bulk field has no well-defined value on such a line.
Networks and interior manifolds currently run in serial. \VERIFY{status at submission}
The resolution along the manifold is that of the parent mesh, and drift-dominated transport is not stabilised.

The main uncertainty lies in the exchange law.
For most interfaces and grain boundaries, the rates are not measured, and are available from atomistic calculations only for a few systems.
Future work will combine a manifold with the kinetic interface channels of the companion paper.
The application to measured microstructures will be investigated in a future study.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
% ===============================================================

This work presents a co-dimensional description of thin features in the hydrogen transport code \FESTIM{}.
A manifold of one dimension less than the mesh carries its own transport equation and exchanges a flux with the bulk.
The two are solved monolithically.
The implementation converges at second order against a manufactured solution, in 2D and on a tilted manifold in 3D.

Three applications were presented.
A permeator tube with the liquid carried on a manifold reproduced published extraction efficiencies of \num{0.690} and \num{0.415}.
The efficiency along the tube was within \num{2e-5} of the analytical solution.
An interface described as a manifold reproduced molecular dynamics results at Be/BeO within \qty{6.3}{\percent}, and a fourteen-site rate-equation model at W/Cu within \qtyrange{3}{19}{\percent}.
In a W/Cu bilayer, the interfacial inventory left the steady flux unchanged within \qty{1.4}{\percent}.
However, it delayed breakthrough by a factor of \num{3.5e4} for \qty{6}{\micro\metre} of tungsten at \qty{340}{\kelvin}.
Finally, the grain-boundary network of a Voronoi polycrystal was described as a single manifold, which carried 31 times the flux of the grains.

The interface and grain-boundary cases are demonstrations on published or assumed parameters, and are not validations against permeation data.
The exchange rates remain the main uncertainty of such models.
Nevertheless, a co-dimensional description allows rates obtained at the atomic scale to be used in component-scale simulations without resolving the feature in the mesh.
